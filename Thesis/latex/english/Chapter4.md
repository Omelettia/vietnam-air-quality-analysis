# CHAPTER 4: ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION

Chapter 3 described the methodology: the data sources and quality control, the sixty-six-feature engineering pipeline, the tier-stratified modeling scheme and its circular dependency, the three deployable proxy approaches, and the regional Red River Delta model. This chapter analyzes what those models actually learned and why they behave as they do. Section 4.1 dissects feature importance, establishing that spatial interpolation from neighboring ground stations dominates the model's predictive power. Section 4.2 evaluates the three chemical transport model products against Vietnamese ground observations and documents the distinct ways in which each fails. Section 4.3 presents a satellite-only ablation that isolates how much of the ground-station signal can be recovered from remote sensing alone. Section 4.4 validates the two existing satellite-derived PM2.5 maps — GHAP and MERRA-2 — against thirty-seven ground stations and shows that both share the same failure mode at clean sites. Section 4.5 argues that station density, expressed through the distance-decay of prediction skill, is the binding constraint on spatial accuracy. Together these analyses explain the central result committed in Chapter 1: a model that reaches an R² of approximately 0.80 under random cross-validation but only approximately 0.20 under leave-one-station-out validation, and they clarify what would and would not move that number.


## 4.1 Feature Importance Analysis

Understanding which features drive the model's predictions is essential for interpreting the validation gap and for judging which signals are genuinely available at unmonitored locations. The definitive model's feature importance is measured as XGBoost normalized gain, in which the contribution of each of the sixty-six features is expressed as a fraction of the total reduction in loss attributable to splits on that feature; the values across all features sum to unity, so the share reported for any group is a direct fraction of total model gain. The analysis below draws on the two most recent importance files, definitive_v3 and definitive_v2 (see analysis/thesis_experiments/definitive_v3_importance.csv and definitive_v2_importance.csv), which agree closely on group-level shares and differ only in the mid-pack ordering of individual features.

### 4.1.1 The Dominance of Spatial Interpolation

The single most informative finding is the dominance of the spatial-interpolation (RFSI) group. In the definitive_v3 model, the eight RFSI features — the three nearest-neighbor PM2.5 values (PM25_nn1, PM25_nn2, PM25_nn3), their inverse-distance-weighted average (PM25_nn_idw), the temporal lags of the nearest neighbor (PM25_nn1_lag1h, PM25_nn1_lag3h, PM25_nn1_lag6h), and the distance to the nearest station (dist_nn1) — account for 34.4% of total model gain, with the earlier definitive_v2 model giving a closely comparable 32.3%. When the single land-use predictor building_area_1km (2.6% of gain), which co-locates with the spatial structure of the network, is grouped together with the spatial features as a measure of the built environment surrounding each station, the spatial-plus-static share rises to approximately 37.0% — the figure cited in Section 3.4.1. RFSI is therefore unambiguously the most important feature group in the model.

The single most important feature is PM25_nn_idw, the inverse-distance-weighted average of the three nearest neighbors' PM2.5, which alone accounts for 11.55% of total gain in definitive_v3 (10.27% in v2) — more than the entire temporal-cyclical group combined. The next two positions are also RFSI features, PM25_nn1 at 6.75% and PM25_nn2 at 4.97%. The dominance is even more pronounced at the very top of the ranking: among the twenty most important features, which together capture 60.4% of total gain, the RFSI/spatial group contributes 54.5% of that top-tier gain — a substantially larger share than its 34.4% overall, indicating that the model leans most heavily on spatial neighbors precisely where it is most confident. This pattern is the feature-importance signature of the validation gap discussed in Chapter 1: a model whose strongest predictor is the PM2.5 of nearby stations will perform well wherever the network is dense and will degrade sharply wherever a held-out station has no near neighbor, which is exactly the behavior quantified in Section 4.5.

### 4.1.2 Satellite, Meteorology, and Temporal Contributions

The second-largest group is direct satellite observation, comprising the MAIAC AOD center and grid statistics, the MODIS Angström exponent, the TROPOMI trace-gas columns (NO₂, SO₂, CO, HCHO), the land surface temperature statistics, and the composite emission proxy smart_v1. This group accounts for 30.7% of total gain in definitive_v3 and 32.5% in definitive_v2 — so close to the RFSI share that in the v2 model the satellite group very slightly exceeds it. Meteorology (rainfall, planetary boundary layer height, ventilation coefficient, wind, temperature, relative humidity, pressure, stagnation hours, and dry-day counts) contributes 22.4% in v3 (22.9% in v2). The temporal-cyclical group — the sine and cosine encodings of month, day-of-year, and hour, together with the weekend indicator — contributes 9.8% (9.6% in v2). The land-use share, consisting solely of building_area_1km, is 2.6%. These shares are stable across the two model versions, indicating that the broad partition of predictive power among spatial, observational, meteorological, and temporal information is a robust property of the data rather than an artifact of any single training run.

The composition of the top twenty features fills in the texture of these group shares. Beyond the three leading RFSI features, the upper ranking interleaves seasonal and observational signals: month_cos ranks fourth (3.32%), the satellite emission-contrast feature smart_v1_contrast sixth (3.12%), the lagged nearest-neighbor PM25_nn1_lag1h fifth (3.15%), and the meteorological persistence features rain_sum_48h (2.19%) and PBLH_min_24h (2.08%) appear at ranks nine and ten. The TROPOMI sulfur-dioxide features so2_contrast and so2_center, the AOD neighborhood statistic AOT_outer_mean, and several smart_v1 components round out the satellite presence in the top twenty. This ordering tells a coherent physical story: the model first locates a station in space using its neighbors, then refines that estimate using the season (month_cos), the local emission environment (smart_v1, SO₂), recent rainfall washout, and boundary-layer mixing depth — precisely the mechanisms identified as governing PM2.5 in Chapter 2.

### 4.1.3 Feature Stability and the Role of Regime Fingerprints

A useful diagnostic of what the model has stably learned is the comparison of feature rankings between the v2 and v3 versions. The top five positions — PM25_nn_idw, PM25_nn1, PM25_nn2, month_cos, and PM25_nn1_lag1h — are invariant across both versions, as are building_area_1km and rain_sum_48h further down. The features that shift rank are predominantly satellite features: so2_center falls from rank ten to rank eighteen, smart_v1_center from fourteen to nineteen, and hcho_center from twenty-eight to thirty-four, while month_sin and the longer neighbor lags climb. In other words, the spatial and seasonal backbone of the model is rank-stable, whereas the satellite contribution, although it remains the second-largest group in aggregate, is distributed across an interchangeable set of trace-gas and emission features whose individual ordering is sensitive to the training configuration. This is consistent with satellite observables carrying genuine but somewhat redundant information about a station's emission regime, no single feature of which is indispensable.

This observation connects to the station regime-fingerprint concept introduced in Section 3.4.6. Those features — thirty-day rolling statistics of satellite observations at each station, such as aod_30d_mean_stn and co_30d_std_stn — were designed to encode "what kind of place this is" from satellite time series alone, and in the experimental run v5b eight of them appeared among the top twenty by gain with a combined importance of approximately 28%, nearly matching the RFSI contribution. It is important to be precise about the scope of that result. The regime-fingerprint features are not present in the definitive production model whose importance is analyzed above; the 28% figure is a property of the v5b experiment, in which the fingerprints were explicitly added as candidate predictors. The two findings are complementary rather than contradictory: when the rolling satellite fingerprints are made available to the model they absorb a large share of gain, demonstrating that satellite time series do encode station-level regime information, but the definitive model achieves its reported performance without them, relying instead on the instantaneous satellite observations and spatial neighbors documented in Sections 4.1.1 and 4.1.2. That the regime information can be captured from satellite statistics yet is insufficient to resolve the tier circular dependency (Section 3.5) is the central tension this thesis characterizes.

The role of the satellite signal becomes clearest when the spatial neighbors are removed entirely. In alternate feature-set configurations that exclude all RFSI features, the satellite and meteorological signals rise immediately to the top of the ranking: a satellite-and-meteorology-only configuration places the wind anomaly WS_anom, the AOD neighborhood mean AOT_outer_mean, the AOD mean AOT_mean, and the boundary-layer anomaly PBLH_anom in its top five (see feature_importance_config_f.csv), and a configuration with land-use and meteorology but no ground neighbors leads with longitude, month_cos, and AOT_outer_mean (feature_importance_config_c.csv). The fact that AOD-based features become the strongest non-spatial predictor as soon as the neighbor signal is withdrawn confirms that satellite remote sensing carries the bulk of the deployable information at locations without nearby ground stations — the very locations the model exists to serve. What the model learns, in sum, is to interpolate from ground neighbors where they exist and to fall back on satellite aerosol and emission signals where they do not, a division of labor that the ablation study of Section 4.3 quantifies directly.


## 4.2 Evaluation of Chemical Transport Model Products

Three reanalysis and chemical-transport-model PM2.5 products were included in the feature set and evaluated as independent estimators over Vietnam's monitoring network: GEOS-CF, MERRA-2, and the GHAP satellite climatology. As foreshadowed in Section 3.4.4, feature-importance analysis found that these products contribute little and that their removal sometimes improves performance. The direct evaluation in this section explains why: all three fail as standalone estimators over Vietnam, but each fails in a characteristically different way, and the conflation of their failure modes in the early outline is corrected here against the recomputed evidence (see analysis/thesis_experiments/satellite_products/).

### 4.2.1 GEOS-CF: An Inverted Diurnal Cycle

GEOS-CF exhibits the most physically diagnostic failure. Its diurnal cycle is inverted relative to ground observations: across 224,365 station-hours matched at the three-hourly cadence of the product, the correlation between the GEOS-CF diurnal shape and the observed diurnal shape is r = −0.14, an anti-correlation. Observed PM2.5 peaks in the early morning at around 06:00 local time (24.0 µg/m³) and troughs near midday (18.8 µg/m³ between 12:00 and 15:00), a shallow cycle with an amplitude of only 5.2 µg/m³, or about 24% of the daily mean. GEOS-CF does the opposite, reaching its minimum at 06:00 — exactly when observed concentrations peak — and climbing to its maximum near 21:00, with an exaggerated amplitude of 43.0 µg/m³, roughly 73% of its own mean (Figure 4.1). This near-antiphase relationship is the unmistakable signature of an emission inventory and boundary-layer scheme tuned to a different diurnal emission pattern: the model appears to schedule its emission and mixing peaks for the wrong hours of the Vietnamese day, most plausibly because its underlying inventory does not capture the early-morning motorbike commute and shallow nocturnal boundary layer that produce Vietnam's observed dawn peak.

Observed concentrations peak near 06:00 and trough at midday with a shallow 5.2 µg/m³ amplitude, whereas GEOS-CF reaches its minimum at 06:00 and its maximum near 21:00 with an exaggerated 43.0 µg/m³ amplitude, the signature of an emission-and-mixing schedule tuned to the wrong hours of the Vietnamese day.*

Beyond the timing error, GEOS-CF grossly overestimates absolute concentrations. Its mean per-station bias is +244% (median +146.6%), corresponding to a mean absolute bias of nearly +37 µg/m³ and an n-weighted pooled bias of +171%; at the most extreme station the overestimate reaches +877%. The resulting per-station R² is strongly negative (mean −22.6) and the per-station mean correlation is only 0.20 (see geoscf_station_metrics.csv and the bias and scatter panels in Figure 4.2 and Figure 4.3). It is worth noting that this +244% overestimate is, in fact, GEOS-CF's bias and not MERRA-2's; the figure was attributed to MERRA-2 in an early draft, but the recomputation places it squarely with GEOS-CF. The combination of an inverted diurnal cycle and a two-to-three-fold high bias renders GEOS-CF unusable as an absolute prior for Vietnam, although its features remain available to the model as a potential source of relative spatial structure that the boosted trees may attempt to correct.

### 4.2.2 MERRA-2: Mass-Balanced but Temporally Unskilled

MERRA-2 presents a contrasting profile. Rather than the catastrophic overestimate of GEOS-CF, MERRA-2 is approximately mass-balanced overall: its mean per-station bias is +38% but its median is −13%, its mean absolute bias is a slight net −1.4 µg/m³, and it actually underestimates at twenty-two of forty stations. Its central failing is temporal: the pooled hourly correlation with ground observations is only r = 0.28, the per-station mean correlation is 0.18, and the per-station mean Index of Agreement (Willmott's d) computed over 241,259 matched station-hours is 0.42 (pooled 0.51). This computed agreement is broadly consistent with the published global-validation figure cited in Section 3.2.4, which reports a Southeast Asian Index of Agreement of 0.39 — the worst of any global region in the MERRA-2 reanalysis. The small numerical difference between the cited 0.39 and the 0.42 recomputed here reflects the difference between the published global-region statistic and this dataset's specific station sample; both indicate weak temporal skill. MERRA-2's representative scatter and its regional species composition (Figure 4.4 and Figure 4.5) show that while the product reproduces the broad north–south gradient in aerosol mass, it does not track the hour-to-hour and day-to-day variability that a useful estimator must capture. MERRA-2 is therefore not grossly biased in the mean, but it is too temporally inert to serve as an hourly estimate, which is consistent with the marginal feature importance documented in Section 4.1.

### 4.2.3 GHAP: Moderate Spatial Skill, Clean-Site Floor

The GHAP annual climatology, by contrast, has the best spatial skill of the three products. Across the thirty-seven retained stations, GHAP's annual values correlate with the true station means at a Pearson r of 0.70 (Spearman ρ = 0.66), with a modest overall high bias of +3.4 µg/m³ (pooled +14.7%) and an overall RMSE of 10.5 µg/m³ (see satellite_grouping_classifier.csv). When stations are sorted into the four pollution tiers used throughout this thesis — clean (<10 µg/m³), low (10–20), moderate (20–35), and high (>35) — GHAP correctly assigns the tier of twenty-one of thirty-seven stations, a concordance of 56.8%, broadly matching the approximately 50% concordance committed in Chapter 1. The errors are not random: GHAP correctly classifies eight of nine high-tier and eight of ten low-tier stations but only one of seven clean-tier stations, systematically pushing the cleanest stations upward into the moderate tier. GHAP thus possesses a useful spatial gradient but rests on an inflated floor at clean rural sites, a limitation examined in detail in Section 4.4.

### 4.2.4 Synthesis

The three products fail in three different registers — GEOS-CF in diurnal phase and absolute magnitude, MERRA-2 in temporal variability, and GHAP in its clean-site floor — but the common thread is that all three were developed and calibrated primarily on dense East Asian and Western data and do not transfer cleanly to Vietnam's tropical, monsoonal, motorbike-and-biomass emission environment. This is precisely why, as reported in Section 3.4.4, the CTM features contribute minimally to the definitive model and their removal is at worst neutral and sometimes beneficial. The practical implication is that none of these global products can be treated as a trustworthy prior for hourly PM2.5 in Vietnam without local correction, and the model's reliance on ground neighbors and direct satellite observations rather than on CTM output is a rational response to their documented deficiencies.


## 4.3 Satellite-Only versus Ground-Station Ablation

The feature-importance analysis of Section 4.1 established that ground neighbors dominate where they exist and satellite signals dominate where they do not. The ablation study in this section quantifies that division directly by progressively adding feature groups and measuring the leave-one-station-out R² at each rung (see satellite_only_v2_test.csv and satellite_only_test.csv, each comprising four configurations evaluated over forty stations with per-station and per-tier metrics). It is worth correcting one structural point from the early outline at the outset: the experiment is a four-rung ladder, not five, and the tier-stratified station embedding and PM history are applied to every configuration rather than constituting a separate rung. There is consequently no isolated "+T4F" step in the ablation.

### 4.3.1 The Ablation Ladder

The ladder begins with a weather_base configuration of twenty-two features (meteorology, temporal encodings, and building density) and progresses through sat_full (sixty-five features, adding the CTM products, AOD, TROPOMI trace gases, emission proxies, and daily anomalies), then sat_v2 (sixty-nine features, adding CTM interaction terms), and finally full_rfsi, the reference configuration that adds the ground-neighbor RFSI features. The mean hourly LOSO R² rises from 0.034 at weather_base to 0.085 at sat_full and 0.079 at sat_v2, then to 0.163 at full_rfsi. Two facts stand out. First, the addition of satellite features roughly doubles the mean R² over weather alone, confirming that remote sensing carries real, non-trivial spatial information. Second, the addition of ground neighbors roughly doubles it again, confirming that RFSI contributes at least as much as all satellite features combined — the same hierarchy observed in the feature-importance shares. The CTM interaction terms added in sat_v2 do not improve the mean over sat_full, and in the older file the CTM-only configuration sat_ctm actually scores below weather_base (0.023 versus 0.029), reinforcing the Section 4.2 conclusion that CTM products add little.

These mean values must be read alongside the medians, because the LOSO means are dragged down by a handful of badly negative stations. On the median, the gap between satellite and ground configurations nearly closes: the median hourly R² is 0.040 at weather_base, 0.096 at sat_full, 0.100 at sat_v2, and 0.105 at full_rfsi. In other words, for the typical station the satellite-only configuration recovers almost all of the achievable skill, and the large mean advantage of ground neighbors is concentrated at a minority of stations where the neighbor signal is decisive. Both the mean and the median should therefore be reported together to avoid overstating the ground-station advantage at the typical site.

### 4.3.2 Where Satellites Match Ground Stations, and Where They Do Not

Two specific claims from the project's earlier analysis reproduce cleanly against these files. First, the satellite-only configuration recovers 72% of the ground-station configuration's skill at the high-pollution tier t3: the mean hourly R² at t3 is 0.405 for sat_full against 0.563 for full_rfsi, a ratio of 71.9% (rising to 72.5% using the best satellite configuration). The polluted tier is where every configuration performs best, and at this tier satellites are nearly as informative as ground neighbors — a meaningful result, since t3 comprises the urban and industrial stations of greatest public-health concern. One framing caveat is warranted: this 72% is a ratio of raw R² values, and because weather alone already reaches R² = 0.347 at t3, the satellite-specific skill above the weather baseline is a smaller fraction (roughly 27%) of RFSI's skill above weather. The thesis reports the raw-R² framing while noting that much of the t3 skill is attributable to meteorology common to all configurations.

Second, there exist stations where the satellite-only configuration outperforms the ground-neighbor configuration. The count is approximately twelve of forty — it reproduces exactly as 12/40 for sat_full in the older file and for sat_v2 in the newer file, though sat_full in the newer file gives fifteen, so the precise number is configuration-dependent and best stated as roughly twelve. Critically, these stations are almost all at the clean and low tiers t0 and t1, not at t3: in the newer file all fifteen such stations fall in tiers t0, t1, or t2 and none in t3, and in the older file only one of twelve is a t3 station. The interpretation is that satellites beat ground neighbors at isolated, clean stations — sites where the nearest training station is distant and the IDW-interpolated neighbor value is therefore a poor estimate — but essentially never at the polluted urban tier, where dense networks make the neighbor signal decisive. The thesis is careful not to claim that satellites outperform ground stations at the polluted tier; the opposite is true, and RFSI dominates t3.

### 4.3.3 Interpretation

The tier-level ladder makes the geography of skill explicit. At t3 the configurations score 0.347 (weather), 0.405 (satellite), and 0.563 (RFSI); at t2 they score 0.120, 0.154, and 0.281; and at the clean tiers t0 and t1 every configuration produces a negative LOSO R², reflecting the difficulty of spatial prediction at rural sites with low concentrations, weak signals, and few near neighbors. The ablation therefore confirms the picture from feature importance: satellites supply a substantial, deployable signal that is most valuable at isolated clean stations and recovers most of the achievable skill at polluted stations, while ground neighbors supply the decisive increment at well-connected polluted sites. Neither source alone is sufficient, and the model's combined performance is bounded above by the density of the ground network it can interpolate from — the constraint analyzed in Section 4.5.


## 4.4 Validation of Existing PM2.5 Maps

Section 4.2 evaluated the CTM products as hourly estimators. This section evaluates the two existing satellite-derived PM2.5 maps — the GHAP annual climatology and MERRA-2's station-mean aggregate — as static maps against the thirty-seven ground stations, the use case for which they are most often cited in exposure assessment. The finding is that both maps share a single, consequential failure mode: they fail at the cleanest sites in exactly the way the model does, by overshooting low concentrations and losing the ability to discriminate among them.

### 4.4.1 GHAP at Clean versus Polluted Sites

At the aggregate level GHAP appears serviceable, correlating with station means at r = 0.70 (R² of the correlation 0.49), with an overall RMSE of 10.5 µg/m³ and a modest overall high bias of +3.4 µg/m³ (see satellite_grouping_classifier.csv and the station-ranking panel in Figure 4.6). This aggregate fit, however, masks a sharp dependence on pollution level. Splitting the stations at 15 µg/m³, GHAP is nearly unbiased at the twenty-four polluted sites (mean bias +0.2 µg/m³, RMSE 9.0, within-group r = 0.68) but overshoots the thirteen clean sites by a mean of +9.3 µg/m³ — nearly doubling the true level, as the observed clean-site mean of 9.9 µg/m³ is mapped to a GHAP mean of 19.2 µg/m³. Eleven of the thirteen clean stations are overstated, against ten of twenty-four polluted ones, so the clean-site bias is roughly forty-six times the polluted-site bias. More damaging still, GHAP carries no usable signal within the clean subset: the within-clean correlation between GHAP and observed station means is −0.13, and after excluding a single station whose GHAP value is a spurious zero (an evidently missing or masked grid cell) the within-clean correlation is −0.58. GHAP not only inflates clean concentrations but actively misorders them.

![Figure 4.6: GHAP station ranking versus observed station means](figures/ghap_station_ranking.png)

*Figure 4.6: GHAP annual values ranked against observed station means across the thirty-seven retained stations. Although GHAP correlates well overall (r = 0.70), it overshoots the cleanest sites by a mean of +9.3 µg/m³ and carries no usable signal within the clean subset (within-clean correlation −0.13, or −0.58 after excluding a spurious zero), inflating and misordering the lowest-concentration stations.*

### 4.4.2 MERRA-2 Shares the Same Failure Mode

MERRA-2, evaluated as a static map via its station-mean aggregate over the matched thirty-seven-station set (with the three broken sensors removed for an apples-to-apples comparison), exhibits the identical clean-site pathology (see merra2_station_metrics.csv). Its overall station-mean bias is only −2.1 µg/m³ and its overall correlation 0.43, but disaggregated by tier it overshoots the thirteen clean sites by +10.5 µg/m³ — mapping an observed clean mean of 9.7 µg/m³ to 20.2 µg/m³, with twelve of thirteen stations overstated and a within-clean correlation of −0.71 — while under-predicting the twenty-four polluted sites by −9.0 µg/m³ (within-group r = 0.74). The magnitude of the clean-site overshoot, roughly +10 µg/m³, is almost identical between the two products. In hourly terms, MERRA-2's per-station bias is +135% at clean sites against −26% at polluted sites, confirming that the strongly positive clean-site error is a robust property rather than an artifact of aggregation. The two products differ at the polluted end — GHAP is approximately unbiased there while MERRA-2 under-predicts — but they converge on the same clean-site behavior.

### 4.4.3 Implications: From Single-Value Maps to Confidence-Aware Mapping

The shared clean-site failure of both existing maps is the same limitation this thesis's own model exhibits, and for the same structural reason. At clean rural sites the genuine PM2.5 signal is small relative to retrieval and reanalysis noise; products built primarily from satellite AOD inherit a positive floor where the aerosol column is dominated by elevated layers or by retrieval artifacts rather than by surface concentration, and any map that reports a single deterministic value at such sites both overshoots the level and loses the ordering among them. The practical recommendation that follows is that PM2.5 maps for data-sparse tropical regions should be confidence-aware rather than single-valued: each grid cell should carry an explicit uncertainty that widens precisely where the supporting evidence is weak — far from ground stations and at low concentrations — rather than presenting an unqualified estimate that is most confidently wrong exactly where it is least supported. This reframing, from a single-value map to a map with spatially varying confidence, is the natural way to communicate the model and product limitations documented throughout this chapter, and it motivates the deployment recommendations of Chapter 6.


## 4.5 Station Density as the Binding Constraint

The preceding sections converge on a single explanation for the validation gap: the model's strongest signal is spatial interpolation from ground neighbors, satellites only partially substitute for that signal, and existing maps fail wherever ground support is weakest. The unifying constraint is therefore the density and reach of the ground network. This section examines that constraint directly, first through the per-area density comparison and then, more decisively, through the distance-decay of prediction skill in the external low-cost-sensor validation.

### 4.5.1 The Density Comparison in Context

The most obvious way to express the network gap is a per-area density comparison, but it must be read carefully. The roughly forty regulatory stations used here span 331,000 km², a density of about one station per 8,275 km², while China's network of more than 1,500 stations across roughly 9.6 million km² — about twenty-nine times Vietnam's land area — achieves roughly one per 6,400 km². Expressed per unit area these densities are 12.1 and 15.6 stations per 100,000 km² respectively, so on the chosen figures Vietnam is only about 1.3 times sparser than China — a modest gap, not the order-of-magnitude difference that the raw count of forty against fifteen hundred might suggest. (China's true operational network exceeds the conservative 1,500 figure used here, so the real gap is somewhat larger, but it remains of the same order.) The density ratio alone is therefore a weak quantitative basis for the "binding constraint" claim. The stronger evidence is empirical and comes from how prediction skill behaves as a function of distance to the nearest training station.

### 4.5.2 Distance Decay of Prediction Skill

The external validation of the regional Delta model against forty held-out sites — thirty-nine low-cost sensors and the US Embassy reference station — provides the decisive evidence (see delta_v1_lcs_validation.csv). Each held-out site carries a recorded distance to its nearest KK training station, and prediction skill decays clearly and significantly with that distance. The robust rank correlation between per-site R² and distance-to-nearest-KK is Spearman ρ = −0.62 (p < 0.001), and the Pearson correlation is −0.43 (p = 0.007); excluding the single 43-km site whose R² of −14.75 is an extreme outlier, the Pearson correlation is a more modest but still significant r ≈ −0.34 (p ≈ 0.034), confirming that the decay is not an artifact of that one point. Binned, the effect is stark. Sites within 10 km of a training station achieve a mean R² of 0.55 (median 0.65), with 94% of them positive. Sites beyond 15 km collapse to a median R² of 0.32 — and a mean dragged to −0.57 by a single extreme outlier 43 km from any training station; excluding that outlier, the far-bin mean is +0.27 and the median +0.36. The median, the more honest summary given the outlier, falls from roughly 0.65 within 10 km to roughly 0.32 beyond 15 km: the model loses half its skill over a span of just a few kilometers of additional isolation. This is the density constraint expressed in the units that matter — not stations per area, but the achievable accuracy as a function of how far a prediction point sits from the nearest anchor.

Read as a whole, the external validation is healthy where the network supports it. Across the thirty-nine low-cost sensors the median R² is 0.53 with 85% of sites positive and roughly half exceeding R² = 0.5, and the US Embassy reference site reaches R² = 0.68 — the headline external-validation figures committed in Chapter 1. These figures should be read as a nearest-station feature-transfer plus sensor-agreement test rather than as genuine spatial prediction at unseen sites: the satellite, AOD, TROPOMI, and ERA5 meteorology features at each held-out site are drawn from the nearest regulatory (KK) station's hourly record, and only the target PM2.5 comes from the held-out low-cost sensor, while the RFSI spatial anchors are correctly localized. The distance-decay therefore partly reflects growing feature-substitution error as the borrowed KK features become less representative with distance, not spatial skill alone. The mean R² of 0.03 is far lower than the median only because a small number of far and degraded sites carry strongly negative scores; six low-cost sites have negative R², five of which lie beyond 15 km from any training station. This separation between a strong median and a weak mean is itself the density story: within dense coverage the model agrees closely with the held-out sensors, and outside it the predictions degrade toward uselessness.

A caution on data provenance underpins this analysis. The distance-decay result is drawn solely from the clean delta_v1_lcs_validation.csv set, which records the distance-to-nearest-KK needed for the analysis. A separate and broader external_validation.csv file covering seventy-seven sites lacks any distance column and includes degraded reference sensors with absurd negative R² values, giving a non-comparable median hourly R² of −0.02 with only 48% of sites positive; the two files are different experiments and are not conflated here.

### 4.5.3 The Path Forward: Densification

If distance to the nearest anchor is the binding constraint, then the most direct lever on accuracy is densification. The value of densification is a projection from the distance-decay curve rather than an instantiated result: the eighty-three low-cost sensors used in this thesis serve only as validation targets and are not anchors in the RFSI neighbour field, which is built from regulatory stations alone, so no observed gain from adding them can be reported here. What the distance-decay curve does imply, quantitatively, is the expected payoff of densification: because skill within 10 km is high and collapses beyond 15 km, adding anchors so that no populated location sits more than roughly 10 km from a sensor would, by extrapolation along that curve, move the bulk of the country into the regime where the model already achieves a median R² above 0.5. This is a far more tractable and inexpensive intervention than tripling the regulatory network, and it points directly to the practical recommendation, developed in Chapter 6, that Vietnam pursue a hybrid network of sparse reference-grade stations densified by calibrated low-cost sensors. The binding constraint on satellite-augmented PM2.5 prediction in Vietnam is not the algorithm and not the satellites; it is the reach of the ground network, and that reach can be extended.


This chapter has analyzed what the model learns and why it performs as it does. Feature-importance analysis revealed that spatial interpolation from ground neighbors dominates predictive power, with satellite observation a close second and the chemical-transport-model products contributing almost nothing. The CTM evaluation documented three distinct failure modes — GEOS-CF's inverted, exaggerated diurnal cycle and +244% bias, MERRA-2's temporal inertness, and GHAP's clean-site floor — explaining the products' marginal feature importance. The ablation study quantified the division of labor between satellites and ground stations, showing that satellites recover roughly 72% of ground-station skill at the polluted tier and outperform ground neighbors only at isolated clean sites. The validation of existing maps showed that GHAP and MERRA-2 share a clean-site overshoot of approximately +10 µg/m³ and motivated a shift toward confidence-aware mapping. Finally, the distance-decay of external validation skill identified station density — measured as proximity to the nearest anchor rather than stations per area — as the binding constraint, with skill collapsing beyond roughly 15 km. Chapter 5 now presents the full quantitative experimental evaluation across all three validation frameworks, reporting the random, leave-one-station-out, and external validation results in detail and comparing every deployable configuration explored in this thesis.
