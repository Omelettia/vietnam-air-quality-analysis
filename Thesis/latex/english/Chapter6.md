# CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSIONS

This thesis set out to answer a single, deceptively simple question: can the satellite-augmented machine-learning approach that reports R² values above 0.8 for China, the United States, and Europe deliver comparable spatial prediction accuracy for Vietnam, given a far sparser network than those it was developed on? The preceding chapters answered that question in the negative for the headline figure but in considerable detail for everything underneath it. Chapter 4 explained what the model learns and why it behaves as it does; Chapter 5 presented the full quantitative evaluation across three validation regimes. This concluding chapter draws those results together. Section 6.1 synthesizes the findings of the entire thesis and ties each back to the four contributions committed in Chapter 1. Section 6.2 translates those findings into concrete recommendations for Vietnam's air quality monitoring strategy. Section 6.3 gathers the principal limitations that qualify these conclusions, Section 6.4 sets out the directions for future work that the results most directly motivate, and a brief closing paragraph restates the central thread.

## 6.1 Summary of Findings

The central result of this thesis is a validation gap, and it is the substance of the first contribution. The same XGBoost-DART model that achieves an out-of-fold R² of approximately 0.80 under five-fold random cross-validation — the figure directly comparable to the published literature for densely monitored regions — falls to a per-station mean R² of approximately 0.20 under honest leave-one-station-out validation, a collapse of roughly 0.60. Because every test hour in the random regime belongs to a station the model has already seen, that figure measures temporal interpolation under station-identity leakage rather than spatial prediction at an unmonitored location; roughly three-quarters of the celebrated accuracy is leakage rather than generalization. This is the first spatially rigorous validation of satellite-based PM2.5 prediction for Vietnam, and the magnitude of its gap is consistent with, and extends to a tropical sparse-network setting, the validation critique documented internationally: Kawano et al. (2025) saw 0.85 fall to 0.67 with roughly a thousand Indian stations, Meyer et al. (2018) saw 0.90 fall to 0.24, and Ploton et al. (2020) saw 0.53 fall to 0.14. Vietnam's gap is larger than India's precisely because Vietnam's network is far sparser, and the immediate implication is that the headline numbers reported for Southeast Asian and other sparse-network PM2.5 models, where random cross-validation remains the norm, very likely overstate real spatial prediction capability by a comparable margin.

The most important interpretive finding, and the heart of the third contribution, is that predictability in Vietnam scales with pollution level, and that the technique exploiting this fact carries a circular dependency that cannot be deployed. Stratifying the thirty-seven training stations into four tiers by their mean PM2.5 and training a separate model for each tier — the T4F scheme — is the single largest performance lever discovered in this study, lifting the LOSO per-station mean from essentially zero under a tier-blind global model to the headline 0.20, a gain of approximately +0.17 (within-file, against the same-file no-grouping baseline of 0.027) to +0.26 — most defensibly about +0.17 — that exceeds any feature group, hyperparameter choice, or ensembling strategy explored. The per-tier breakdown is monotone and unambiguous: the highest-pollution tier t3 reaches a mean R² near 0.56, the moderate tier t2 near 0.31, while the low and clean tiers t1 and t0 sit at or below zero, the model being unable to beat the station's own mean where the variance to be explained is itself small. That this level dependence is the operative mechanism, rather than geography, can only be advanced as a hypothesis here, because level and region are confounded at the top of the distribution: all nine t3 stations are Northern, so the apparent regional gradient and the level gradient cannot be separated in the highest tier. The only place they can be compared at fixed level is t2, where the two Ho Chi Minh City sites outscore most northern stations of similar concentration and the southern t2 mean R² (0.459, n=4) exceeds the northern (0.278, n=4); but with only four stations per side the difference is not statistically significant (Welch p=0.30, Mann-Whitney p=0.34, 95% CI [−0.13,+0.49]), so it is suggestive rather than confirmatory and cannot establish that region is irrelevant. The dependency is circular because assigning a station to its tier requires knowing its mean PM2.5, which is exactly the quantity the model exists to predict at an unmonitored location.

The price of that circular dependency defines the deployable ceiling, the second half of the third contribution. When the oracle tier is withdrawn and only genuinely observable satellite, meteorological, and land-use features are allowed, performance collapses from the 0.20 LOSO figure to a per-station median near 0.04. Three independent deployable approaches — the soft-gate mixture of experts, the two-phase predicted baseline, and the satellite-derived regime fingerprints — converge at the upper edge of a near-zero positive band, strong evidence that the genuine deployable performance from satellite observables alone, absent local calibration, is consistent with a near-zero positive band whose upper edge is approximately +0.04 median. Above that ceiling sits the oracle: injecting each held-out station's true mean as an additive base margin lifts the per-station mean to roughly +0.27 with about 92% of stations positive. The gap between the deployable band and that oracle ceiling — roughly +0.28 to +0.33 in per-station mean R² and some forty percentage points of usable stations — is the precise information cost of not knowing a station's mean. Seven distinct families of methods were developed specifically to break the dependency — soft tier boundaries, GHAP-hybrid interactions, tier ensembling, two-phase tier prediction, observable-based clustering and nearest-neighbour routing, invariant-feature removal, and anomaly reframing — and all seven failed to reach the true-tier figure, every deployable variant falling at or below it. These negative results are not an afterthought; they triangulate the boundary of what is achievable and establish that no satellite-observable proxy tested reliably resolves the dependency. The deployable +0.04 ceiling is therefore an information ceiling for *observable estimation* of the baseline, imposed by the sparsity of the ground network rather than by any deficiency of effort or algorithm.

The dependency does, however, admit a resolution — not from observables, but from the monitoring network itself. The seven families all sought the station's mean in observable feature space; the eighth approach, spatial-prior routing, instead interpolates it from physical space, anchoring each held-out station's baseline to the distance-weighted observed means of its neighbours and routing target-free prediction streams toward that estimate. With GHAP removed, this fully deployable pipeline reaches a per-station mean R² of 0.197 against the oracle's 0.203 — closing almost the entire deployable-to-oracle gap that the observable proxies could not — and does so with zero high-to-non-high and zero dangerous low-to-high station flips, marginally safer than the oracle itself. On forty-six unseen low-cost sensors it retains a per-station mean near 0.20 with no clean site mapped as polluted, generalizing as an elevated-pollution screening map. This does not contradict the +0.04 ceiling; it specifies it. The baseline is unrecoverable from satellite observables and recoverable by spatial interpolation, so the achievable deployable skill is not a single number but a function of proximity to the network: near-oracle where the target has usable neighbours, collapsing toward the observable floor where it does not. The binding constraint is, once more, the reach of the ground network. The model remains a heavy, feature-rich research pipeline rather than an operational map — it was not engineered for live continuous-coverage inference — so the contribution is the demonstrated *resolution principle*, not a deployed product.

The systematic evaluation of global PM2.5 products — the second contribution — provides the complementary negative result and explains why those products cannot supply the missing information. All three reanalysis and chemical-transport-model products fail as standalone estimators over Vietnam, but each fails in a characteristically different register. GEOS-CF exhibits a diurnal cycle inverted relative to ground observations, reaching its minimum at the dawn hour when surface PM2.5 peaks, together with a mean per-station bias exceeding +244% — the unmistakable signature of an emission inventory and boundary-layer scheme tuned to a different diurnal pattern. MERRA-2 is approximately mass-balanced in the mean but temporally inert, with a per-station Index of Agreement near 0.39 to 0.42, consistent with its published status as the worst-performing of any global region. GHAP carries the best spatial gradient of the three, correctly assigning the pollution tier of only about half the stations, but rests on an inflated floor at clean rural sites, overshooting the cleanest concentrations by roughly +10 µg/m³ and actively misordering them. Evaluated as static maps, both GHAP and MERRA-2 share this same clean-site pathology — the identical failure mode the thesis's own model exhibits and for the same structural reason: where the genuine surface signal is small relative to retrieval and reanalysis noise, products built primarily from satellite AOD inherit a positive floor and lose the ability to discriminate among low concentrations. The common thread is that all three were calibrated primarily on dense East Asian and Western data and do not transfer cleanly to Vietnam's tropical, monsoonal, motorbike-and-biomass emission environment. None can be treated as a trustworthy prior for hourly PM2.5 without local correction, which is precisely why the definitive model relies on ground neighbours and direct satellite observations rather than on CTM output, and why the CTM features contribute essentially zero gain, their removal being neutral or beneficial.

The external validation — the fourth contribution — supplies the positive counterpart to these negative results and pinpoints the true binding constraint. It is best understood as a nearest-station feature-transfer plus sensor-agreement test rather than as genuine spatial prediction at unseen sites: for each held-out low-cost sensor the satellite, AOD, TROPOMI, and ERA5 meteorology features are drawn from the nearest regulatory (KK) station's hourly record, and only the target PM2.5 comes from the sensor itself, while the RFSI spatial anchors are correctly localized. Tested in this way against thirty-nine independent low-cost sensors and the reference-grade US Embassy monitor, none of which were used in model development, the regional Red River Delta model achieves a median R² of 0.53 across the low-cost sensors with about 85% of sites positive, and R² = 0.68 at the US Embassy's beta-attenuation monitor — the single most credible external result in the thesis, though it is partly a feature-transfer result and should be read as such. These figures are competitive with the best published spatial cross-validation results internationally and close the gap with the Indian benchmark within dense coverage, where the median R² of sites within 10 km reaches 0.64. The wide gap between the strong median and the weak mean is the now-familiar signature of a few far, degraded sites, and it is itself the density story: prediction skill decays clearly with distance to the nearest anchor (the distance-decay relationship derived in Sections 4.5 and 5.3), a decay that partly reflects the growing feature-substitution error incurred as the nearest regulatory station's meteorology stands in for conditions further away. Feature importance confirms the mechanism — spatial interpolation from ground neighbours (RFSI) accounts for the largest share of model gain at approximately 37%, satellite observation a close second near 31%, meteorology around 22%, temporal encodings near 10%, and CTM output essentially nothing — so the model's strongest predictor is, by construction, the PM2.5 of nearby stations. The unifying conclusion across all four contributions is therefore singular and quantitative: spatial prediction skill in Vietnam is bounded above by the reach of the ground network rather than by the algorithm or the satellites. The model's spatial capability is limited by station density, and that density can be extended.

## 6.2 Recommendations for Vietnam's Air Quality Monitoring

The findings of this thesis translate into four concrete recommendations for how Vietnam should generate, validate, and communicate satellite-based PM2.5 estimates.

First, leave-one-station-out or equivalent spatial cross-validation should be adopted as the field standard for evaluating any PM2.5 prediction model intended for deployment at unmonitored locations. The central result of this thesis is that random cross-validation overstates spatial prediction capability by roughly 0.60 in R² for a sparse network, and that the inflation is not a quirk of one model but a structural consequence of station-identity leakage that any model trained on temporally autocorrelated ground data will exhibit. A reported R² obtained by shuffling hourly observations without regard to station identity measures temporal interpolation at known stations, not spatial prediction at new ones, and the two differ by a factor that grows precisely as the network thins. For a country as sparsely monitored as Vietnam, the honest figure is the only operationally meaningful one. Procurement specifications, regulatory acceptance criteria, and academic claims of spatial accuracy should all require the model to be retrained with the evaluation station excluded from both the training set and the spatial-neighbour pool, and should report the per-station median and the fraction of stations with positive R² alongside any pooled figure, since each summary alone is systematically misleading.

Second, global chemical-transport-model and satellite PM2.5 products — GEOS-CF, MERRA-2, and GHAP among them — must not be used as uncorrected priors for Vietnam, whether as input features weighted on faith, as exposure surfaces for health assessment, or as gap-fillers where ground data are absent. The systematic evaluation in this thesis showed each product failing in a distinct register, and the failures are most severe at exactly the clean rural sites where, lacking ground stations, a planner would most want to trust a global map. A product that inflates a true 10 µg/m³ to 20 µg/m³ and misorders the cleanest sites does not merely add noise; it manufactures confident error precisely where it is least supported. Where these products are used at all, they should enter only after explicit local bias correction against Vietnamese ground measurements, and their absolute values — particularly GEOS-CF's inverted diurnal cycle and several-fold high bias — should never be reported as PM2.5 estimates without that correction.

Third, and most consequentially, Vietnam should pursue a hybrid monitoring network of sparse reference-grade stations densified with calibrated low-cost sensors, with the explicit planning target that no populated area sits more than approximately 10 km from an anchor. The distance-decay evidence is unambiguous: within 10 km of a training station the model is competitive with the best international results, and beyond 15 km its predictions degrade toward uselessness. The low-cost sensors that came online in late 2025 served only as independent validation targets in this thesis, not as interpolation anchors, so the densification case rests on projection rather than on a demonstrated gain: extrapolating the measured distance-decay curve, extending anchor coverage so that the bulk of the populated country falls within the 10 km regime would, on that projection, move most of Vietnam into the band where the model already achieves a median R² above 0.5. Adding calibrated low-cost sensors to the spatial-neighbour pool is the natural way to realize such coverage, but the magnitude of the resulting gain remains to be demonstrated empirically. This is a far more tractable and inexpensive intervention than multiplying the regulatory network. The reference-grade stations remain essential as calibration and quality-control anchors against which the low-cost sensors are corrected, but it is the reach of the densified network, not the count of regulatory instruments, that governs achievable accuracy.

Fourth, the operational products derived from such a network should be published as confidence-aware maps with spatially varying uncertainty rather than as single deterministic values. The thesis demonstrated that both the model and the existing global products are most confidently wrong exactly where they are least supported — far from ground stations and at low concentrations — and that a single-value map cannot communicate this. Each grid cell should instead carry an explicit uncertainty that widens with distance from the nearest anchor and at low concentration, so that a prediction in a well-anchored polluted area is presented as reliable while a prediction in an isolated clean area is flagged as uncertain. This reframing converts the documented limitations of the method from a liability into honest, actionable information for the public-health and regulatory users who depend on the maps, and it follows directly from the density constraint that governs the underlying skill.

## 6.3 Limitations

Several limitations qualify the conclusions above and are gathered here by reference rather than re-derived. The external low-cost-sensor validation is the most heavily caveated result: as set out in Section 6.1, it is a nearest-station feature-transfer plus sensor-agreement test rather than genuine spatial prediction at unseen sites, since every satellite, AOD, TROPOMI, and ERA5 meteorological feature for a held-out sensor is substituted from the nearest regulatory station's record while only the target PM2.5 is independent; part of both the headline external R² and the distance-decay it exhibits is therefore attributable to feature substitution. That validation is also confined to the dry season, leaving an unquantified seasonal confound, and rests on short laser-scattering low-cost records whose stability over longer periods is untested. The strong external mean is further driven by a small number of sites and is sensitive to a single outlier, so the median is the more trustworthy summary, and the headline figures are computed on the feature-complete subset of sites for which all inputs were available rather than on the full sensor population.

The remaining limitations concern the ground network and the reporting conventions. The regulatory network is small — thirty-seven stations nationwide, twelve in the delta model — which both constrains the achievable spatial reach and limits the statistical power of every per-tier and per-region comparison, as the non-significant t2 North–South contrast in Section 6.1 illustrates. Throughout the thesis the per-station summaries are reported as n-weighted means and medians of per-station R² rather than as a single pooled R² computed over all hours at once; as noted in Section 5.1 these two conventions need not agree, and the per-station figures are the operationally meaningful ones but are not interchangeable with a pooled value. These limitations are candidly acknowledged: none overturns the central validation-gap finding, but each bounds how far the more favourable external and regional figures should be generalized.

## 6.4 Future Work

The results of this thesis point to several specific directions for further research, organized around the central finding that the deployable ceiling is set by the information cost of not knowing a station's mean PM2.5.

The most direct line of work is to extend the spatial-baseline estimator beyond the reach of the current network. This thesis already took the decisive step the earlier outline anticipated only as future work: the spatial-prior routing pipeline (Sections 3.6.4 and 5.5) estimates a station's baseline by interpolating the observed means of its neighbours and, used to anchor and route target-free streams, closes almost the entire deployable-to-oracle gap (per-station mean 0.197 against the oracle 0.203). The limitation of that estimator is now sharply defined: it works where the target has usable neighbours and decays where it does not, so the open problem is no longer recovering the baseline near the network but estimating it where the network is absent. Two complementary routes follow. The first is a richer observable station-mean estimator — incorporating finer land-use classification, gridded population and built-environment density, road-network and traffic-emission proxies, and bottom-up emission inventories rather than the coarse satellite climatologies tested here — to raise the baseline estimate in regions the spatial prior cannot reach; the two-phase satellite regression of this thesis managed only a leave-one-out R² of about 0.59, and improving it is the path to extending coverage into sparsely monitored areas. The second is to re-engineer the pipeline itself: the current model is a heavy, multi-stream, feature-rich research artifact, not an inference engine, and turning the demonstrated resolution principle into a continuous-coverage operational map would require distilling it into a single deployable estimator light enough to run over a national grid.

A complementary and likely more powerful route is few-shot on-site calibration: collecting a short ground record — on the order of weeks — at a target location to estimate its mean PM2.5 directly, then supplying that empirical mean to the model as a base margin. This thesis established that knowing the true station mean lifts performance to the oracle level of roughly +0.27, and a brief deployment of a calibrated low-cost sensor is the cheapest way to obtain a usable estimate of that mean without a permanent station. Quantifying how short such a record can be before the estimated mean degrades performance — and how the resulting accuracy interpolates between the +0.04 deployable floor and the +0.27 oracle ceiling as a function of record length — is a well-defined and high-value experiment.

A third direction is to formalize the satellite reliability or trust map on a single global model. The thesis showed that a deployable model performs far better at the polluted tier than at clean sites, and that satellite observables can classify a station's regime imperfectly but informatively. Rather than routing predictions through tier-specific models, a single global model could emit a per-location reliability score derived from its satellite-observable regime classification, directly operationalizing the confidence-aware mapping recommended in Section 6.2 and providing the spatially varying uncertainty that single-value maps lack.

Two further avenues follow from the network and data structure. Regional models for dense clusters should be developed beyond the Red River Delta wherever station density permits: the delta model's LOSO mean of 0.302 and median of 0.433 modestly outperformed the deployable tier-blind nationwide configuration on the same stations, supporting the regional strategy and suggesting that any sufficiently dense future cluster — around Ho Chi Minh City as the low-cost network expands southward, for instance — would benefit from a dedicated model that shares meteorology, emission types, and transboundary exposure. Finally, the low-cost-sensor validation in this thesis was confined to the dry season, so the strong external results carry an unquantified seasonal confound; collecting longer, multi-season low-cost records would allow the dry-season figures to be separated from genuine year-round spatial skill and would remove the most important caveat attached to the external validation.

## Closing

This thesis began with the gap between a published number and an honest one, and it ends by having located the source of that gap precisely. The R² of approximately 0.80 that the satellite-augmented model attains under random cross-validation is real but measures the wrong task; under honest leave-one-station-out validation the figure is approximately 0.20, and once the circular dependency on a station's own pollution level is withdrawn, the genuinely deployable estimate from satellite observables alone is a per-station median near 0.04. The chemical-transport-model and satellite products that might have supplied the missing information fail over Vietnam in three distinct ways, and seven families of observable proxy methods could not recover it — but the missing quantity proved recoverable from the monitoring network rather than from the observables: anchoring the baseline to neighbouring stations through spatial-prior routing lifts the deployable per-station mean back to 0.197, within a hair of the 0.203 oracle, and carries to unseen low-cost sensors as a safe screening map. Within dense ground coverage the model achieves a median R² of 0.53 and a reference-grade R² of 0.68, competitive with the best international results. The unifying lesson is that spatial prediction skill for PM2.5 in Vietnam is bounded not by the algorithm and not by the satellites but by the reach of the ground network — the dependency is broken precisely where the network reaches and binds precisely where it does not — a constraint that is, encouragingly, the most tractable one to relax. Densifying that network with calibrated low-cost sensors so that no populated area lies far from an anchor, validating honestly, and mapping with explicit confidence together constitute a practical and affordable path from the deployable floor documented here toward the accuracy that Vietnam's air quality monitoring will require.
