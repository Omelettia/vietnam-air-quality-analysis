# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Problem Statement

Air pollution is one of the most pressing environmental and public health challenges facing Vietnam. According to the IQAir 2024 World Air Quality Report, Hanoi recorded an annual average PM2.5 concentration of 45.4 µg/m³ — more than nine times the World Health Organization's guideline of 5 µg/m³ and nearly double Vietnam's own national standard (QCVN 05:2013) of 25 µg/m³. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5), defined as airborne particles with aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 micrometers, is the air pollutant most strongly associated with adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, and premature mortality. The Global Burden of Disease study estimates that ambient PM2.5 exposure contributes to over 4 million premature deaths worldwide each year, with Southeast Asia among the most affected regions.

Despite the severity of the problem, Vietnam's usable ground-level PM2.5 monitoring is sparse and geographically uneven. This study draws on 40 regulatory stations operated by the Vietnam Center for Environmental Monitoring (CEM) that provided sufficient continuous hourly PM2.5 over the 2023–2025 study period (37 after quality control; see Section 3.3), concentrated in the northern Red River Delta and thinning sharply across the centre and south. Vietnam's national network is larger but still modest — the government's environmental-monitoring master plan targets 201 automatic air quality stations by 2030, up from roughly one hundred today, and only a subset of these, chiefly in the centrally-run cities, measure PM2.5 rather than the regulated gases (SO₂, NO₂, CO, TSP) alone (VietnamPlus, 2024). By comparison, the studies reporting R² above 0.85 for China, the United States, and Europe were trained on national networks of many hundreds to thousands of stations — China's alone exceeds 1,500 — one to two orders of magnitude more training anchors than were available here. This sparse and uneven coverage means that the vast majority of Vietnamese citizens — particularly those in rural areas, small cities, and industrial zones outside major metropolitan centres — lack access to local air quality information, and, as Chapters 4 and 5 establish, it is the resulting distance from any location to its nearest usable station, rather than the nationwide station count or stations-per-unit-area, that ultimately constrains spatial prediction accuracy.

Satellite remote sensing offers a potential solution to this monitoring gap. Instruments aboard polar-orbiting and geostationary satellites observe the Earth's atmosphere at spatial resolutions of 1–10 km, providing daily or sub-daily measurements of aerosol optical depth (AOD), trace gas concentrations, and land surface properties that correlate with surface-level PM2.5. When combined with meteorological reanalysis data and machine learning algorithms, these satellite observations can, in principle, be used to estimate ground-level PM2.5 concentrations at locations without monitoring stations. This approach has been widely adopted in the literature, with numerous studies reporting high prediction accuracy (R² > 0.8) for regions such as China, the United States, and Europe. The central question this thesis addresses is whether similar performance can be achieved for Vietnam, given its sparse monitoring network, tropical climate, and unique emission sources.


## 1.2 Current Solutions and Limitations

The satellite-based PM2.5 estimation literature has grown rapidly over the past decade. Studies in China, using networks of over 1,500 ground stations, have demonstrated R² values exceeding 0.85 with machine learning models such as random forests, XGBoost, and deep neural networks, incorporating features from MODIS AOD, ERA5 meteorology, land use data, and chemical transport model (CTM) outputs. Similar results have been reported for the United States and Europe, where dense monitoring networks provide abundant training data.

However, a critical examination of these results reveals a systematic methodological concern. The vast majority of published studies validate their models using random k-fold cross-validation, in which individual observations are randomly split into training and test sets. Because ground monitoring data exhibits strong temporal autocorrelation — PM2.5 at a given station at hour t is highly correlated with the same station at hour t-1 — random splitting allows the model to effectively "see" each station during training and merely interpolate its temporal patterns during testing. This is fundamentally different from the operationally relevant task of predicting PM2.5 at a location where no ground measurements exist.

Recent studies have begun to quantify this discrepancy. Kawano et al. (2025), published in Science Advances, applied both random and spatial cross-validation to a PM2.5 model for India and reported R² of 0.85 under random cross-validation but only 0.67 under spatial cross-validation — a drop of 0.18 in absolute terms. Meyer et al. (2018) documented even larger gaps of up to 0.66 (R² from 0.90 to 0.24) in a spatio-temporal prediction task — an environmental-variable case study on air temperature (with a companion soil moisture case falling from 0.92 to 0.49), cited here as general methodological support for the spatial cross-validation critique rather than as a PM2.5-specific precedent. Ploton et al. (2020), published in Nature Communications, demonstrated that spatial validation revealed "quasi-null predictive power" (R² = 0.14) for a biomass mapping model that achieved R² = 0.53 under standard non-spatial validation. These findings suggest that the headline performance numbers in much of the PM2.5 literature may substantially overstate real-world spatial prediction capability.

For Vietnam specifically, the published literature is limited. Ngo et al. (2023) developed a mixed-effects model for Vietnam using 9 monitoring stations over 2012–2020, reporting R² = 0.75. However, this result was obtained using sample-based cross-validation, and the small number of stations raises questions about spatial generalizability. Several studies have addressed PM2.5 prediction in neighboring countries — Thailand, the Mekong River Basin — but none have applied rigorous spatial cross-validation (leave-one-station-out or equivalent) to a Vietnamese dataset.

Additionally, global PM2.5 products derived from satellite data, such as GHAP (Global High-resolution Air Pollutants) and MERRA-2 (Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2), are increasingly used for exposure assessment in data-sparse regions. However, these products have not been systematically validated against ground measurements in Vietnam, and their accuracy in tropical, monsoonal environments with unique emission profiles — motorbike-dominated traffic, biomass burning, small-scale industry — is unknown.


## 1.3 Objectives and Solution Direction

The primary objective of this thesis is to develop and rigorously evaluate a satellite-augmented machine learning model for estimating hourly ground-level PM2.5 concentrations across Vietnam. Unlike previous studies that rely on random cross-validation, this thesis employs leave-one-station-out (LOSO) cross-validation as the primary evaluation metric, ensuring that reported performance reflects genuine spatial prediction capability at unmonitored locations.

The specific objectives are as follows. First, to construct a comprehensive feature set integrating multiple satellite and reanalysis data sources — including MODIS MAIAC AOD, Himawari-8/9 AOD, TROPOMI trace gases, ERA5 meteorology, GEOS-CF and MERRA-2 chemical transport model outputs, GHAP PM2.5 climatology, nighttime lights, and building density — and evaluate the contribution of each source to prediction accuracy. Second, to quantify the discrepancy between random cross-validation and spatial cross-validation performance on the same model and dataset, contextualizing the results within the broader literature on validation methodology. Third, to investigate the role of pollution regime stratification in model performance and to characterize the circular dependency that arises when the grouping variable is itself the prediction target. Fourth, to validate the model against an independent network of low-cost sensors and a reference-grade US Embassy station not used in training. Fifth, to evaluate existing global PM2.5 products against Vietnamese ground measurements and assess their suitability as spatial priors.

The modeling approach is based on XGBoost with DART (Dropouts meet Multiple Additive Regression Trees) regularization, chosen for its strong performance on tabular geospatial data and its ability to handle missing values — a practical necessity given that satellite AOD retrieval is available for only approximately 15% of hours due to cloud cover in Vietnam's tropical climate.


## 1.4 Contributions

This thesis makes four main contributions:

(i) It presents the first spatially rigorous validation of satellite-based PM2.5 prediction for Vietnam, demonstrating that the same model achieves R² of approximately 0.80 under random cross-validation but only approximately 0.20 under leave-one-station-out validation — a finding consistent with, and extending to a tropical sparse-network context, the validation critique documented by Kawano et al. (2025), Meyer et al. (2018), and Ploton et al. (2020).

(ii) It provides a systematic evaluation of chemical transport model products for Vietnam, revealing that GEOS-CF exhibits an inverted diurnal PM2.5 cycle relative to ground observations together with a mean bias exceeding 200%, that MERRA-2 — although approximately unbiased in the mean — achieves an Index of Agreement of only 0.39, the worst among all global regions, and that GHAP achieves only approximately 50% concordance with station-level pollution tier assignments — documenting that global products calibrated primarily on East Asian and Western data perform poorly in Southeast Asian tropical environments.

(iii) It identifies, characterizes, and resolves a fundamental circular dependency in pollution-regime-stratified prediction: stratifying stations by PM2.5 level improves model R² by approximately 0.21, but requires a priori knowledge of the variable being predicted. Through systematic testing of multiple proxy methods — soft gating, predicted baselines, satellite-derived regime fingerprints, GHAP-based assignment, emission clustering — the thesis demonstrates that no satellite-observable proxy reliably resolves this dependency, with all observable-based deployable approaches converging on a per-station median R² of approximately 0.04. It then shows that the dependency is nonetheless resolvable from the monitoring network rather than from observables: a spatial-prior routing model that anchors each station's baseline to the observed means of its neighbours recovers a deployable per-station mean R² of 0.197, against an oracle ceiling of 0.203, with zero dangerous low-to-high misclassifications, and generalizes to unseen low-cost sensors as a safe screening map. The resolution is conditional on station proximity, which makes the achievable deployable skill a function of network density rather than a single ceiling.

(iv) It validates the model against 39 independent low-cost sensor stations and one US Embassy reference station not used in training, achieving R² = 0.68 at the Embassy and a median R² = 0.53 across LCS stations within dense network coverage — results competitive with the best published spatial cross-validation results internationally and demonstrating that the model's spatial prediction capability is primarily limited by station density. These external figures are obtained from dry-season-only low-cost sensor records under a nearest-station feature-transfer setup (the satellite and meteorological predictors are drawn from the nearest regulatory station rather than measured at the sensor site), and should be read as a feature-transfer and sensor-agreement result rather than as genuine spatial prediction at the sensor location.


## 1.5 Thesis Structure

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.

Chapter 2 presents the theoretical background necessary for understanding the satellite-based PM2.5 estimation problem. It reviews the physical basis of the AOD-PM2.5 relationship and the factors that complicate this relationship in tropical environments. It surveys the machine learning approaches used in the literature, with emphasis on XGBoost and the DART regularization strategy. It also provides a detailed treatment of spatial validation methodology, explaining why random cross-validation inflates reported accuracy and how leave-one-station-out validation provides a more honest assessment of spatial prediction capability.

Chapter 3 describes the proposed methodology in detail. It covers the study area and monitoring network — 40 regulatory stations, 83 low-cost sensors, and one US Embassy station — and the multi-source satellite and reanalysis datasets used. It presents the feature engineering pipeline that produces the predictor variables, the data quality control procedures that identified and removed malfunctioning sensors, the tier-stratification scheme, and the deployable approaches developed to address the circular dependency. It also describes the regional model applied to the dense Red River Delta subnetwork.

Chapter 4 provides a detailed analysis of the experimental results. It examines feature importance patterns across pollution tiers, revealing that spatial interpolation from neighboring stations dominates model predictive power while satellite-derived regime fingerprints provide complementary information. It evaluates the performance of chemical transport model products for Vietnam and presents a satellite-only ablation study. It assesses existing global PM2.5 maps against Vietnamese ground truth and analyzes station density as the binding constraint on spatial prediction accuracy.

Chapter 5 presents the experimental evaluation. It reports results across three validation frameworks — random cross-validation, leave-one-station-out, and external low-cost sensor validation — enabling direct comparison with published literature. It presents tier-stratified results, compares all deployable model configurations, and documents the complete set of experiments explored.

Chapter 6 concludes the thesis with a summary of findings, practical recommendations for Vietnam's air quality monitoring strategy, and directions for future work.
